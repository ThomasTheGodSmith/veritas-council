Market Council

A council of AI models, each trained by us from scratch, that stress-tests a planned investment in the Dow Jones before a first-time investor puts money in. It can, and often should, say "don't buy".

This project is a prototype and is not financial advice.

The idea

Who it's for, and what goes in and out

The user

A 17-year-old with S$500 who wants to buy "the Dow", or one Dow company like Apple. They have a hunch and some rules about how much they can afford to lose.

What goes in

  • A ticker: DJIA (DIA) or one of the 30 members
  • An amount, e.g. S$500
  • Hard rules, e.g. max loss S$50 · 20 trading days · no banks
  • Their own idea: "I think it'll go up"

What comes out

  • A verdict: BUY S$350, WAIT or DON'T BUY
  • A plain-English report showing which models agree with the user and which challenge them
  • One folder per model, holding its own decision and reasons
  • Two buttons: approve (places a paper order) or decline (give a reason, and the council re-runs)

Does a council of self-trained models make safer decisions than any single model, a simple majority vote, or simple rules like buy-and-hold?

This is our own question, which the brief rewards. We expect "always buy" to be hard to beat on accuracy, because the Dow has risen in most 20-day windows since 1992. The council's realistic win is safety: fewer broken loss limits and smaller drawdowns. If the honest answer is "not much", we report that.

How a decision is made

From the user's plan to a verdict, in order

1

Historian gathers the evidence

A store that only ever returns data dated before the decision day, plus every model's past predictions and a "find similar past situations" search.

storage · never votes
2

Round 1: every analyst predicts on its own

Eleven models each give a probability that the price is higher in 20 trading days, and say whether they support or challenge the user's idea.

11 voting analysts + a Fundamentals info card
3

Round 2: cross-examination

For each pair that disagrees today, the Historian checks who was right when they disagreed like this before. The Devil's Advocate model estimates how often the majority is wrong in situations like this one.

the debate, done with data
4

Checklist

Code checks every hard rule (✅ or ❌, and nothing can overrule a ❌). It also lists the positives and negatives of each analyst from its real track record.

rules are code, not a model
5

Mediator

A trained stacking model learns how much to trust each analyst, and when. It combines them into one probability and builds 1–3 plans, possibly including its own, such as a split entry. Any plan that breaks a rule is fixed or dropped.

trained on the validation years
6

Judge

Buy only if the council's probability clears a threshold, the expected return is positive and every rule passes. The amount uses half-Kelly sizing, capped by the Risk Officer's maximum safe amount.

threshold tuned on validation, never on test
7

Report, then the user decides

Claude Opus 5.5 turns the finished results into a readable report. It never sees market data or news and cannot change the decision. Code checks that every number it writes matches the models' output, and falls back to a template if one doesn't.

✅ Approve & Invest → paper order❌ Decline + reason → back to step 2
The council

Eleven analysts, each looking at the market a different way

Every model is trained from scratch on our datasets, on the training years only. No pretrained models (no FinBERT, no LLMs) make any prediction, so none of them can already "know" what happened in the test years. The number is the build order: if time runs short, the lowest priorities are cut first.

How we checked it

The Dow is price-weighted: the index equals the sum of its 30 members' real prices divided by a "divisor". If our membership table and split-corrected prices are right, that divisor should stay flat. It does: 0.168 today, moving a median of 0.0009% a day. It jumps only on real events, like Apple joining in 2015, Nike's 2-for-1 split and Alphabet joining in June 2026.

Gaps we'll report

  • Companies that went bust or were bought out (Sears, Texaco, Kodak, Walgreens…) have no price history. Only 20 of 30 members have prices in the 1990s, and 29–30 in our test years.
  • Company news stops in 2020, so in the test years News-Reaction uses world headlines only.
  • CPI and jobs numbers are published weeks late, so we shift them to their release date to avoid leakage.
How we test it

Train on the past, test on years no model has seen

Splits are by date, never random, with one cutoff for every dataset, set by the Dow's 35 years (1992–2026). The newest 20% of years (7) is the test set, and the rest is training. The newest ~15% of the training years is a validation slice for tuning the Mediator, Devil's Advocate and Judge. Training and test data are written to separate folders. Training code can only open the training folder, so it doesn't have the test years at all. Tests check this every run.

Every 5 trading days in the test window, for the Dow and for each company that was a member on that date, a fixed example user ("wants to buy, max loss 10%, 20 days") gets a buy / wait / avoid decision. Each decision is scored on what the price actually did over the next 20 days.

Baselines the council must beat

  • Always buy (buy-and-hold)
  • Same direction as the last 20 days
  • Buy only if the price is above its 50-day average
  • The best single analyst on its own
  • A simple majority vote of the analysts, with no Mediator, Devil's Advocate or debate. This is the key comparison.

What we measure

  • Accuracy, and the Brier score (are the probabilities honest?)
  • Average return of "buy" decisions, Sharpe ratio, worst drawdown
  • % of decisions that broke the user's loss limit, the safety number
  • How often challenging the user's idea turned out right
  • Ablations: with vs without the Devil's Advocate, stacking vs simple average, Pattern Oracle vs Raw Reader
  • At least one case where the council was confidently wrong, and why
Ground rules

What keeps the results honest and the user safe

  • No look-ahead. Anything given a decision date only sees data from before it, with a unit test that proves it.
  • Fit on training years only: model weights, scalers, word vocabularies, token bins, the HMM and GARCH.
  • Training prices are frozen as of 31 Dec 2019. Yahoo adjusts old prices for splits that happened later, which quietly encodes the future, so the training folder undoes that.
  • Point-in-time membership. In 2010 the council only knows the 2010 Dow, not today's winners, which avoids survivorship bias.
  • Real prices for weights. Apple's 4-for-1 split cut its Dow weight by about 75% overnight, so we undo split adjustments.
  • No live trading. Orders go only to Alpaca's paper endpoint or a local simulator. Real brokers need users to be 18+.
  • Claude never predicts. It only writes the report from finished results, and the app works without it.
  • The Judge can't override a broken rule. A ❌ on the checklist blocks the plan.
  • No hiding weak results. If a test result beats the baselines by a suspicious margin, we look for leakage first.
